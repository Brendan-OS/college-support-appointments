##Technological Based

It's likely that an app would be an overkill and overly complex solution. However, depending on the size of the college and the number of appointments being made or requested, it could be considered.

An app would have several benefits, such as:
- Easy to amend.
- Easy to advertise.
- Reduces the workload of reception staff.
- 
Its disadvantages would include:
- Expensive to develop and maintain.
- Numerous requirements for full functionality.

I would only realistically consider an app-based solution to be feasible for a larger college, one that needs to deal with potentially a huge number of appointments in a single day. In a smaller environment, the cost-efficiency would generally be too low.

Additionally, depending on the current appointment booking system, a technology-based solution could build upon existing infrastructure, which would significantly improve the ease of implementation. An example of this would be if the college already uses an online booking platform.

##Old-school Based

Using paper and forms to organize meetings and appointments would be feasible for a small-scale school, but it would be highly inadvisable to implement this approach in a college-level environment.

It could operate fine for in-society meetings, but the Society Officer(s) would likely face difficulties trying to keep a large number of papers and folders containing sensitive information organized and secure.

There is also the added concern of privacy. A sheet of paper posted on a noticeboard, similar to a sign-up chart, is publicly visible and may expose a student's appointment to others when they do not wish for it to be made public.

The simplicity of paper should not be overlooked despite its flaws. It could still be utilized alongside the chosen solution to support its operation. Paper can also be shredded, so the ability to temporarily store sensitive information before destroying it is worth considering in its own right. Another advantage is that paper does not depend on network connectivity or electricity to remain accessible.

Most data is often stored alongside a physical copy, even in modern environments. These copies can be difficult to locate and even harder to access without the relevant permissions. This inconvenience is often avoided by staff because physical copies are a secondary form of data storage that they do not need to access very often, as most information is securely stored digitally.

##Email Based

A system designed around stakeholders sending emails to arrange appointments would be preferable for a medium-sized college, rather than either of the previously suggested solutions.

However, this system would place responsibility on the people arranging meetings, such as receptionists and their superiors.

While organizing meetings is part of a receptionist's job, a college would typically have hundreds of students looking for appointments, which could potentially become overwhelming. (Although this college's student population is not explicitly mentioned, it can be assumed that it will not have an exceptionally low student population for a college or university, which would still likely be well above 1,000.)

A possible solution would be to teach students the correct format for arranging appointments by email, while also allowing a superior, such as a DSS Officer, to arrange the meetings themselves. The receptionist would simply verify and forward the requests.

This method would also help to identify spam, scams and inappropriate emails. (For example, a student attempting to arrange a meeting with Person A but emailing Person H, or sending an email without following the correct format.)

An important consideration is that the chosen format should not be difficult to understand or follow independently. The usual professional format used when emails are exchanged between lecturers and students should suffice.

This would only act as a supporting method, similarly to the paper-based approach suggested previously.

##Hybrid Based

A combination of several approaches would likely be the most flexible and easiest solution to implement.

For example, a receptionist could receive an email requesting an appointment and then use an app, shared calendar or website to record the appointment so that the relevant meeting participants can view it. (The meeting participants, such as a DSS Officer, would be consulted by the receptionist before anything is confirmed. Furthermore, the other party involved in the meeting would also be consulted to determine whether the proposed date would work for them.)

It is important to note that the more people involved in meetings and the creation of appointments, the more flexible the chosen solution should be.

It may be unrealistic to suggest that every meeting participant within the college (such as Society Officers, DSS Officers and Management personnel) would each have their own receptionist. Therefore, the most practical solution would be for a receptionist to act as the recipient of appointment requests. The receptionist would verify the legitimacy of each request before forwarding it to the relevant meeting participants.

For example:
A student emails to request additional support for a module.
Reception verifies that the student is genuinely enrolled in the college. This is intended to prevent the staff responsible for arranging meetings from being overwhelmed with false requests, which could cause delays. The receptionist then forwards the email chain to the relevant meeting participant.

For students with access to special support services, this would most likely be the DSS Officer. The receptionist could then step back from the email chain and allow the student and relevant meeting participants to arrange a suitable meeting time according to their respective calendars.
Since the student and meeting participants now have direct contact, cancellations and rescheduling could potentially be handled between the two or more parties involved.

The email chain would also make any additional follow-up meetings and queries much quicker to resolve.
